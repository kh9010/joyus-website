Finding the Thesis

Most teams don't have a silo thesis problem.

A one-day offsite for leadership teams that have product-market signal but can't yet say, in one sentence, what the company is and who it's for. You leave with that sentence, two or three customer archetypes, and a 90-day plan with named owners. We've run it inside a Series A fashion startup eight months past launch — we're now running it for other teams.

Here's the pattern we keep seeing. A leadership team books a strategy day because the silos are getting worse. Growth and product describe the same business in different terms. The pitch deck describes it in a third. Someone says the word "alignment" and the room goes quiet because nobody knows what they'd be aligning on. The silos aren't the problem. The missing sentence is.

When there is no shared answer to what are we, for whom, and why, every function fills the vacuum with its own version. Merch fills it with selection. Marketing with content. Product with features. Ops with cost. Teams drift, each one feels alone, and nobody trusts the others' choices because nobody is choosing against the same brief. You don't fix that with team-building. You fix it by giving everyone the same sentence to point at.

"If you have to find a reason to say no, you'll always find one. The work is making the reason to say yes louder than the reasons to say no."

From the workshop

How a day with us runs

Three things, in order: a diagnostic, a thesis, and a 90-day plan.

The diagnostic is two exercises that take an hour and consistently surface what's actually going on. A three-minute round where each leader says why they're spending their life on this company — adjacent answers are normal, identical answers are rare, and the gap between them is where most of the misalignment lives. Then a sticky-notes round: what's working, what's broken in how we work together, and what's a problem you're holding alone. The third question does the most work. It's where the silent stuff comes out.

The thesis is one sentence: who is this for, in what moment of their life, replacing what. Most teams arrive describing themselves with a channel — "quick commerce for fashion," "a community for runners," "an AI agent for legal." That's a description. The work is finding the sentence underneath it. It takes longer than people expect. It's almost always worth it.

"We're not a quick-commerce app. That's a channel. We're the place she goes when she's standing in front of her closet and feels like she's already wearing it wrong."

A founder, halfway through finding the sentence

The 90-day plan is the bridge from the sentence to the work. Two or three customer archetypes you'll win on first. Operating principles for how the team runs experiments — small bets first, fast read on signal, no long-build bets without short-cycle validation. Three to five things to test in the next fourteen days, with named owners and pre-committed win conditions.

By the time everyone leaves, the silos have started to close. Not because anyone gave a speech about collaboration, but because the team finally has the same brief.

what teams keep finding

Three reframes this room almost always surfaces.

  1. People form bonds when they give, not when they take.

    Most teams measure conversion as did they buy — the narrowest possible definition of a relationship. Real stickiness comes from the moments where a user gives back: a profile, a piece of feedback, a photo, an opinion, ten minutes. Companies that ask for nothing get nothing. The workshop almost always surfaces three or four "investment surfaces" the dashboard has been walking past.

  2. The thesis is hiding a tension you're not naming.

    Most theses sit on top of a contradiction the team hasn't said out loud. The marketing voice goes one way; the operating model goes the other. The customer the founder describes in pitch meetings isn't the one the unit economics actually serve. Teams that name the tension get a real choice in how to resolve it. Teams that hide it from themselves keep optimizing both sides into a stalemate.

  3. Differentiation has two halves.

    "We're different — we have feature X" is what every product page says; buyers and investors have heard the line two hundred times. Real differentiation has two halves: a customer opens the product and immediately sees something they don't see elsewhere, and they're compelled enough to mention it to a friend. We pressure-test every claimed differentiator against both halves. Most fail the second.

What you walk out with

  • One sentence the leadership team can say back to each other — who, in what moment, replacing what
  • Two or three customer archetypes you commit to winning on first
  • Operating principles for how you run experiments, written down
  • A 90-day plan with named owners and pre-committed win conditions
  • A written summary of what was decided, so it doesn't quietly get retconned

How to know you're ready

  • You've tried to write the company in one sentence twice in the last quarter and the team didn't agree on either version.
  • Investors say "interesting" but don't move. The deck has been "almost" for two months.
  • You've noticed yourself answering the same internal question — what are we, exactly — three different ways in three different rooms.

If two of those land, book the diagnostic. If all three, skip the diagnostic and book the workshop.

A note on what this isn't

It isn't strategy consulting. We don't show up with a framework and a deck and tell you what your business is. The thesis has to come from the room — our job is to make the room work, ask the questions you've been avoiding, and write down what comes out clearly enough that it survives Monday morning.

It also isn't team-building. There are no trust falls, no values exercises. The exercises we do run are diagnostic, not therapeutic. The team gets closer because the work gets clearer, not the other way around.
