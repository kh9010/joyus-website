the thesis workshop · for leadership teams

Most strategy days produce decks. Ours produces a sentence.

A leadership offsite for teams with product-market signal but no thesis-market fit. We run it as a one-day room, sometimes two — and you leave with the one sentence the rest of the team can say back.

One day · two-day option Leadership team only Pre-thesis-market-fit

Here's the pattern. A team books a strategy day because the silos are getting worse — and what they actually need is a thesis. Growth and product describe the same business in different words. The pitch deck describes it in a third. Someone says "alignment" and the room goes quiet because nobody knows what they'd be aligning on. The silos aren't the problem. The missing sentence is.

"If you have to find a reason to say no, you'll always find one. The work is making the reason to say yes louder than the reasons to say no."

01
9 — 12 · diagnostic

First, what's actually going on.

Two exercises in the morning. A three-minute round where each leader says, in their own words, why they're spending their life on this company — adjacent answers are normal, identical answers are rare. Then sticky-notes on three colors: what's working, what's broken in how we work together, and what's a problem you're holding alone. The third question is the one that does the most work.

Somebody's voice cracks a little on the third sticky-note question — what's a problem you're holding alone. Then somebody else raises their hand and says, "I have that one too."

a reframe this room almost always surfaces

People form bonds when they give, not when they take.

Most teams measure conversion as did they buy — the narrowest possible definition of a relationship. Real stickiness comes from the moments where a user gives back: a profile, a piece of feedback, a photo, an opinion, ten minutes. The diagnostic almost always surfaces three or four "investment surfaces" the dashboard has been walking past.

02
12 — 4 · thesis

Then, the sentence underneath.

Most teams arrive describing themselves with a channel — "quick commerce for fashion," "a community for runners," "an AI agent for legal." Channels are descriptions. The afternoon's exercise is finding the sentence underneath: who you're for, in what moment of their life, replacing what. It always takes longer than people expect. It's almost always worth it.

Someone who's been quiet all morning says a sentence the rest of the room immediately recognizes. The team starts finishing each other's thoughts instead of arguing past each other.

a reframe this room almost always surfaces

The thesis is hiding a tension you're not naming.

Most theses sit on top of a contradiction the team hasn't said out loud. The marketing voice goes one way; the operating model goes the other. The customer the founder describes in pitch meetings isn't the one the unit economics actually serve. Teams that name the tension get a real choice. Teams that hide it from themselves keep optimizing both sides into a stalemate.

03
4 — 6 · 90-day plan

Then, the bridge to Monday morning.

Two or three customer archetypes the team commits to first — named, with real demographic and behavioral specificity. Operating principles for how experiments run, in writing. Three to five things to test in the next fourteen days, with named owners and pre-committed win conditions. The plan that turns the sentence into the next ninety days.

later

The person who'd been the difficult voice in every meeting becomes, very quickly, the right voice. The team realizes the question they'd been brushing off was the question.

a reframe this room almost always surfaces

Differentiation has two halves.

"We're different — we have feature X" is what every product page says; buyers and investors have heard the line two hundred times. Real differentiation has two halves: a customer opens the product and immediately sees something they don't see elsewhere, and they're compelled enough to mention it to a friend. We pressure-test every claimed differentiator against both halves. Most fail the second.

By the time everyone leaves, the silos have started to close. Not because anyone gave a speech about collaboration — because the team finally has the same brief. We can't promise the room shifts. We've watched it shift every time.

Five things in writing.

the sentence

One line the leadership team can say back to each other.

For ____ in the moment of ____, we replace ____.

Who you're for, in what moment, replacing what. The exercise that takes the whole afternoon.

the archetypes

Two or three customers you commit to first.

Named, with real demographic and behavioral specificity. What replaces "25–35, urban metro, high intent."

the principles

How the team runs experiments — in writing.

Small bets first. Fast read on signal. No long builds without a fast version. Define success per surface up front.

Signed off by the room. Most have been violated quietly in the prior quarter.

a quick self-test

How to know you're ready.

  1. You've tried to write the company in one sentence twice in the last quarter and the team didn't agree on either version.
  2. Investors say "interesting" but don't move. The deck has been "almost" for two months.
  3. You've noticed yourself answering the same internal question — what are we, exactly — three different ways in three different rooms.

If two land, book the diagnostic. If all three, skip it and book the workshop.
