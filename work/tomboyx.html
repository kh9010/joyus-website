a case study

tomboyx

Beloved underwear company in need of a refreshed story to raise a re-capitalization in a down round.

  • Three decks
  • Three audiences
United States 10–20 employees $25M annual revenue

A down round is an honest place to start. TomboyX had the metrics of a category winner and the cap table of a company that needed to be re-capitalized — so the job wasn't to invent a story, it was to decide which true one each investor needed. One company, three decks: for the operator who reads a P&L, the believer who funds values, and the syndicate that forwards PDFs to LPs.

beat 01 — how each deck opened

How each deck opened.

for the operator

Operators read an opener like a P&L header, so the operator deck opened with the comparison already done: tomboyx in colour, the market in grey, four bubbles deep. Returns at 2.5% against a 30% market standard. Margins at 72% against 53%. Conversion at 4.3% against 3% — all of it on a 9% brand spend where the market burns 20%. The claim wasn't "trust us." It was: this isn't a turnaround, it's a mispriced company.

for the believer

Believers get told "community" by every brand deck ever made, usually with a mood board attached. We opened the believer narrative from the working file instead — three years of customers, split by income band and by generation, from poverty line to top wealth, Silent Generation to Gen Z. The feeling investors had about tomboyx was real; this was the spreadsheet underneath it. Every band buys. Every generation comes back.

for the syndicate

A sendable deck works with nobody talking next to it, so the opener had to carry the whole identity in one slide. We used the brand manifesto — black slide, plain type: absolute comfort in your skin is what gives you the confidence to make your mark on the world. If an LP read only this far, the sign-off did the job of a tagline and a thesis at once: Fit for You.

beat 02 — how each deck handled the numbers

How each deck handled the numbers.

for the operator

Operators don't want the rounded version — they want the mechanics. So the numbers slide read like a working model: 2.6% return rate and 4.33% conversion for 2023, CAC cut from $49 to $23 in a year, first orders already profitable at 1.5× on a gross-profit basis, and the share of customers arriving through organic channels climbing from 58% to 66%. Footnotes included, because an operator always reads the footnotes.

for the believer

For the believer, the numbers became a fairness argument. A $93B lingerie market growing 6–8% through 2030 — and in it, Meundies raised $50M, ThirdLove $55M, Adore Me was acquired for $400M. The asterisk on the slide did the editorial work: male founded = well funded; tomboyx has been underfunded. Then the yellow banner that turned grievance into pitch: we can do it better and more authentically.

for the syndicate

The syndicate deck held its KPIs to a single highlights slide and spent the saved slide budget on evidence no spreadsheet carries: wedding invitations addressed to the company, a 2025 graduation announcement, a mother's letter about her trans daughter who only wears TomboyX, a handwritten note on orange paper. When your NPS is 76 and the market's is 50, this is what the gap looks like as physical mail.

beat 03 — how each deck framed the broken years

How each deck framed the broken years.

for the operator

Operators respect a diagnosis with a price tag. The operator deck named the issues head-on — misalignment between VC owners and management since 2020, starved working capital, debt service running over a million dollars a year, a board without clear priorities — and gave each its fix on the same slide, arrows left to right. Then it showed where the new money actually goes: 56.3% to demand generation, 28.2% to product development, 9.9% cash buffer, 5.6% market activations. The scar, the patch, and the invoice — one breath.

for the believer

This was the framing decision the whole engagement turned on. Many believers were early investors — people who might read the broken years and assign blame to past leadership, or to decisions they'd contextualize very differently from a new investor. So the deck chose its villain deliberately: market forces drove tomboyx to the breaking point — not past leadership, not decisions anyone in the room had backed. No postmortem, no culprits. And where the operator got a pie chart, the believer got this: a customer with the logo tattooed on her arm. The thing that didn't break.

for the syndicate

An LP reading a forwarded PDF doesn't want the history — they want to know who's holding the wheel now. So the syndicate deck answered "what went wrong" with a team slide: Fran, co-founder and former CEO, back in the pitch; Leslie running it as CEO with Stanford, Starbucks, REI and T-Mobile behind her; Ryan on CFO/COO from Stanley and ZipWhip; Katrine as chief merchant out of Nordstrom and Eddie Bauer. The layout is deliberately spare — minimal focus on the past, all the attention on the team rather than any individual player.

beat 04 — how each deck asked for money

How each deck asked for money.

for the operator

Operators don't fund stories; they fund gaps. So the operator ask sat on a market argument: intimates growing at 8–10% CAGR, $3.46B in 2022 to an expected $6.55B by 2030, while the former category leader gave up 45 points of share — Victoria's Secret from 65% to just 20%. Under it, a five-year plan with EBITDA milestones: a $700K bridge in 2025, $1.8M in 2026, $2.5M+ in 2027, $3.3M+ in 2028, and $30–40M revenue with $5M+ EBITDA by 2029 — exploring roll-up or sale. The ask, in operator terms: buy the gap, hold five years.

for the believer

The believer ask wasn't a term sheet — it was an invitation: join a syndicate of like-minded investors. $5M for a one-third stake, and instead of a fund logo, the slide named the people already committed, LinkedIn links and all. Two of those names are ours — Kahran Singh is on the committed list, alongside Pradeep Singh. We don't write asks we wouldn't answer. At deck time the tally read: commitments so far, $2M of $5M.

for the syndicate

A syndicate close has to survive being screenshot at a red light. So it closed on one slide that holds the entire investment case: product leader — first to launch boxer briefs for women; category pioneer — one-third of units sold in 2X+ extended sizes, certified B Corp, on shelves at Kohl's, REI Co-op, Nordstrom and Amazon; outstanding KPIs — 76 NPS, 55,000+ five-star reviews, 50% repeat rate, returns under 5%, 227k on Instagram. All of it over a strip of customers actually wearing the product. One screenshot, whole argument.
