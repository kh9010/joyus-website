Making the case for wellbeing — to the people who sign the cheque.

A wellbeing product sells to a buyer who has to justify it. So we built Tatsam's pitch to speak to the head and the heart at once — the business case, the human case, and the belief underneath them both. And the same narrative discipline ran every B2B engagement after it: each client got a bespoke data story, built to tell the truth about their organisation without frightening it.

the frame · opening the room

A $50 billion problem, and an incumbent that reaches almost no one.

The pitch was written for the person who signs the cheque, not the person who downloads the app. It opens on the size of the gap — and on how badly the existing answer (the corporate EAP) fails to close it:

$50B

estimated yearly cost of workplace mental ill-health across India & SE Asia.

~5%

of staff actually reached by EAPs — the incumbent answer.

1–2%

EAP adoption. A benefit nobody uses isn't a benefit.

1,032

employees in the proof study placed at the centre of the deck.

Against that gap, the pitch frames a maturity model — organisations move from tensed to flourishing — so a buyer can locate their company on a path instead of in a verdict. Then every intervention is tied to the three outcomes a buyer actually weighs.

the three outcomes

Three outcomes a buyer actually weighs.

The pitch doesn't ask a company to care out of kindness. It shows three kinds of return, side by side.

Business outcomes

The hard case — productivity, retention, the cost of a workforce running on empty.

Mental-health & wellbeing outcomes

The human case — people who feel better, supported before they break.

Positive organisational behaviour

The culture case — teams that show up differently when wellbeing is real, not a perk.

the b2b data stories

Every client got a bespoke data story — informative without being alarmist.

From the work deck, verbatim: "Tatsam's B2B clients required in-depth research for each project, and the data story to be presented in a way to appropriately tell the current state of wellbeing in the organization." That's a storytelling problem as much as a research one — a wellbeing report that frightens leadership gets shelved, and one that flatters them changes nothing. So each report follows the same narrative arc: an overview of the key areas of concern for the team, then the detailed indices, always highlighting the critical areas that require attention — and always ending on what there is to build on.

Under the headline, the indices put numbers a leadership team can act on: emotional issues accounted for 62.2% of the impact on presenteeism and absenteeism, emotional and health issues together for 73.9% of work impairment — and 63% of the workforce reported moderate-to-low meaning in life. On the organisational side, negative emotions alone predicted 9.2% of the variation in performance and productivity impairment.

the closing move · psychological capital

Every data story ends on the upside.

The last panel of every report turns the diagnosis into a buy decision. Psychological capital — "a set of resources individuals can use to help improve their wellbeing, performance and success at work… proven to develop a competitive edge at an individual and organisational level" — is broken into its four constructs, each with its modelled headroom at the client:

+52%

Hope — improves how employees generate pathways to accomplish goals.

+44%

Efficacy — mastery, social cohesion, and a positive, optimistic outlook.

+30%

Resilience — asset-focused strategies & resourcefulness.

+44%

Optimism — a growth-lens on potential risks; consistent personal strengths.

Tell the truth about the problem, then sell the size of the upside. That's the whole storytelling move.

the belief underneath

Built on strengths, not weaknesses.

Every slide rests on the same conviction that runs through the brand and the product: focusing on strengths and not weaknesses. It's what makes Tatsam's pitch feel different from every "fix your broken people" wellness deck.

Research proved the need. Brand gave it a voice. Product made it real. The pitch made it fundable.
