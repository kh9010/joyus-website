Tatsam · Holistic workplace wellbeing · Case study

We didn't fix what was wrong.
We grew what was strong.

In 2021, a founder bet that mental health improves faster when you grow people's strengths than when you catalogue their deficits. Our job was to carry that one bet through every artifact a young company needs — the diagnostic that proved the need on a real workforce, the brand that gave the belief a voice, the code-ready product, and the pitch that framed a $50B problem. Same conviction, four disciplines, no drift.

the bet

Mental health has always asked what's wrong. Tatsam asks what's right.

The whole company rests on one paradigm shift — from a pathology model to a psychological-capital model: tools that help people flourish rather than just function. We didn't invent that belief; the founders brought it. Our job was to make sure it survived contact with every deliverable — that the research wouldn't quietly revert to deficit-hunting, the brand wouldn't drift into clinical comfort-speak, the product wouldn't become another guilt library. Every choice below is that one belief, defended four times.

It builds psychological capital — Hope, Efficacy, Resilience, Optimism — so stronger individuals compound into more resilient organisations.

Hope — goal-setting, planning, action Efficacy — confidence to meet challenges Resilience — bounce back from stress & change Optimism — reinforces self-efficacy & hope

That belief is the through-line of everything below — it shows up in the brand voice, the product's logic, and the pitch's outcomes.

↓ first, is the need even real?
the evidence · research & strategy

We measured wellbeing with four validated instruments — not a vibe.

The category's habit was anecdote — "people seem stressed." We chose to prove the need with instruments a board can't wave off: for a real workforce (Urban Ladder), we ran the PERMA Profiler, the Work Productivity & Activity Impairment scale, the Stanford Presenteeism Scale, and the Flourishing Scale, then scored the org against global benchmarks. It came back below every one (Holistic Wellbeing 32.2, Flourishing 33.3 vs a global mean of 44.5) — and the sharpest finding wasn't where anyone was looking: the executives presumed fine were the least well in the building.

The cost isn't people staying home. It's people showing up depleted — and the leaders steering the company are among the least well.

Underneath the headline, the texture: 60% of employees reported overall wellbeing below the Asian average, two-thirds scored low-to-moderately-low on engagement, one in three reported no real social support at work, and 30% said flatly that they felt lonely. Low happiness alone accounted for a 33% rise in presenteeism and a 67% drop in engagement and positive organisational behaviour.

And the same dataset held the upside. Psychological capital at the org measured 32.2% — with a modelled headroom of +40%. Played through, that one gain reads as:

+40% PsyCap headroom +63% work happiness & satisfaction +22% performance +36% positive org behaviour

The strengths bet wasn't philosophy. It was the largest number in the report.

the voice · brand & identity

A brand built on belief — and a voice that never makes failure feel like failure.

The category default in 2021 was clinical — crisis lines, intake forms, the visual language of being a patient. We built the identity against that grain, on three beliefs: holistic health for all (mind, body, environment), wisdom from east and west, and tradition meets technology. The logo move says it smallest: we took the medical "+" and reflected it — the same mark, turned into "a softer, more gentle way of showing care." Then we wrote the voice hard rules, because a wellbeing brand fails in its sentences first:

"Agency not boundaries." Three voice rules, each with its own never:

Empathetic — neither paternalistic nor enabling Empowering — never making failure feel like a negative Liberating — agency, not boundaries

None of it was decreed from a studio — the guide came out of deep brainstorming sessions and generative workshops with the founders and team, and it runs the whole surface: presence, communication and philosophy, down to recording rules for audio (clear voice, no noise, no music over the instructor) and video (bright, well-lit, short — "short is better"). Two offerings carry it to users: self-care (a self-paced path aimed at changing mindset, not just habits) and coaching (curated coaches across mental counselling, career, physical health and nutrition).

the thing itself · product & interface

Psychological capital, turned into an app you'd actually open.

The wellness category ships libraries — a hundred meditations you'll feel guilty about by Thursday. We designed the opposite, code-ready: an onboarding built in collaboration with psychologists to create intimacy without overwhelm, two ways through — self-driven or guided, because agency was a brand rule before it was a feature — and a recommendation engine that hands you one doable thing. The strengths bet, the voice rules, and the interface logic are the same decision wearing three outfits.

The onboarding is the belief in miniature, four moves long:

01 Rapport — your name, how you feel, space to write about it 02 Life details — satisfaction across 8 areas, career to romance 03 Goals — the user picks the focus, not the app 04 The plan — day one starts easy, on purpose

Plus the marketing site that lands it all in a scroll.

the case · story & narrative

A $50 billion problem, framed for the person who signs the cheque.

We wrote the pitch for the person who signs the cheque, not the person who downloads the app. The number that opens the room: workplace mental ill-health costs an estimated $50B a year across India & SE Asia, while the incumbent answer (EAPs) reaches ~5% of staff at 1–2% adoption. Against that gap we framed a maturity model (tensed → flourishing), put the 1,032-employee proof study at the centre, and tied every intervention to the three outcomes a buyer actually weighs:

Business — productivity, retention, revenue Wellbeing — self-efficacy, fewer sick days Org behaviour — culture, resilience, retention

what mattered

What we learned, that you can take into any company.

  1. One belief, defended at every altitude. The strengths-over-deficits flip only worked because nobody let it dilute — not in the instruments, not in the logo, not in the recommendation engine, not in the cheque-writer's pitch. Cohesion isn't a style choice; it's a discipline.
  2. Prove the need with instruments, not anecdotes. Four validated scales turned "people seem stressed" into a board-ready diagnosis — including the finding that the executives presumed fine are the least well.
  3. Presenteeism is the real cost. People show up depleted; physical health explained only ~a quarter of it. Wellbeing is a productivity line, not a perk.
  4. One belief has to run through everything. "Agency not boundaries" in the voice, "one doable thing" in the product, "strengths" in the pitch — the same conviction, four times.

And the honest ending: Tatsam didn't make it — the company eventually wound down. The bet still reads right; presenteeism is still the unsolved cost sitting on every P&L. Carrying one conviction through every artifact is what gave it a real shot. The market decides the rest — and the work is why we'd take the same brief again tomorrow.
