a case study

gliitch

Gliitch sells digital tools to small restaurants in Hong Kong — a company with a loud personality and a brand too quiet to carry it. The brief had a contradiction built in: stand out in one of the most colorful food markets anywhere, while sharing every flyer, loyalty card and video with a restaurant's own logo. We revitalized the identity to do both, in three moves — moodboard, mark, then the motion rules that keep it alive without us in the room.

memphis energy meets HK street

Tech vendors dress like tech — cool grays, gradient meshes, the visual grammar of software. In Hong Kong's street-level food scene that reads as invisible. So we moodboarded the other way: Memphis-style design language, multiple primary colors for high energy, strong geometric shapes, supporting patterns — loudness as a strategy, not a styling choice.

The brief, in one line from the deck: "Balance vibrance & loudness across different axes — with adaptability."

The word doing the real work is adaptability — because the constraint that disciplined every choice was co-branding, with all of Gliitch's small-restaurant customers, online and digitally. Gliitch's surfaces carry its customers' marks at the center: the cover up top shows a flyer for one of those restaurants — a hummus place — wearing the Gliitch frame — "Spread hummus, not hate" across the top, the restaurant's own mark holding the middle, and the loyalty mechanics underneath: open Facebook Messenger → go to people → scan code, then a reward journey dotted from "extra hummus" to "free meal." The identity has to be loud enough to register and generous enough to frame someone else's logo without swallowing it.

↓ now narrow it — the mark

the logo we landed on

The obvious move for a company called Gliitch is the glitch itself — and two of the four candidates took it literally, letterforms breaking apart at high and low contrast. Tempting, and wrong: a logo that's mid-malfunction can't sit calmly next to a restaurant's mark on a co-branded flyer. The new logo set the tone for the branding instead. With just two colors in the final version, the shapes allude to nostalgic emotional energies — a circle straight out of old TV idents — that stand out in a hyper-future-focused competitive market. The glitch survives, demoted to a detail: a quiet pixel cluster over the double-i.

Logo candidates — explored across container, contrast and circular forms.

The other reason the circle won: it's a container that scales sideways. The first sub-brand, gliitch bites, ships in the same circle — three typographic treatments of "bites" tested under the wordmark, derivation over invention.

↓ then bring it to life — in motion

now it moves

Most brand guides stop at the flat page — and for Gliitch that would have been the expensive mistake, because its customers' content is food video, and that's where the co-brand actually lives. So the guide specifies behavior, not just placement. Extending the branding to video brought a sense of play — elements interacting with each other, and the view space, in a way that was impossible with a simple two-dimensional branding. Videos open with the frame partially covered by the logo and brand color, which slides right to reveal the footage; they end with a screen of all the colored pixels popping out as the video closes — abundance as a sign-off.

And because real food footage is chaotic, the guide writes the brand a retreat plan — three logo placements in descending order of presence: regular logo bottom-right as the default; a white transparent version when active video content needs to take priority; and a non-intrusive dynamic alternative — three boxes in the corner, animating in and out. The co-branding generosity again, now in motion: when the restaurant's content is the point, Gliitch knows how to step back.
