a case study

secret senses

The Indian hair-care shelf splits two ways — pharma-clinical white or heritage-Ayurveda brown. Secret Senses, a bootstrapped Bringraj line, needed a third: an identity that could say nature and India without looking like medicine — and stretch to markets it couldn't yet afford to enter. We answered with a notebook of hand-painted watercolors and the discipline to repeat them.

three decisions, one notebook

Paint it, don't license it.

The category default is stock botanicals — the same vector leaves on every "natural" bottle in the aisle. Secret Senses got a notebook instead: hand-drawn watercolor elements, scanned and vectorized, which — in the deck's own words — "form the heart of the brand." And the flowers aren't decoration: the illustrations chosen for the pattern nod to the key ingredients of the products. The art is the ingredient list, painted.

Repeat it — but let it breathe.

A florid pattern is one bad decision away from wallpaper. The system runs one pipeline — loose elements → tiled pattern → label frame — and holds itself back on purpose:

"With a unique pattern, the brand is reinforced, without overwhelming. Negative space balances the repetition of the brand illustrations and colors."— from the design rationale

On the label, the flowers softly peek through from the edges — embodying the brand value of nature as a foundation, not nature as a costume.

Indian by line work, not by cliché.

No turmeric-gold, no temple borders. India enters through the drawing itself: an ornate S logomark built from floral, paisley-inspired linework "to highlight nature and Indian origins," a wordmark set in Alex Brush — a handwritten font chosen to create familiarity — and one leading teal, #55c2b4, picked because it feels "both clean and fresh, evoking nature but with more sensuality." The tagline under the mark does the rest: discover the secrets of nature.

Build for shelves it isn't on yet.

"Grounding in nature gives an emotional resonance which allows for brand extendability — a key requirement when future markets are on the horizon."— from the design rationale

A bootstrapped team of fewer than five can't rebrand every time it adds a SKU. So the test of the system wasn't the shampoo — it was everything after the shampoo. Same pattern, same frame, same promises, four products deep.

Every label carries the same four promises — sulphate free, paraben free, color protect, no artificial color — in the same quiet type. And the honest footnote: this was an identity, illustration and packaging engagement; the deck claims the system, not the sell-through, and so do we.

a mark, and a palette to hold it all.

The system in its parts: the paisley-built S, the pattern doing label duty, five saturated primaries — "energy and vibrancy ripples through the saturated color palette" — and the secondary tonal scales that let the same flowers whisper or shout.
